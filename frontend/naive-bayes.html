Naive Bayes Classification

Naive Bayes is a supervised Machine Learning model. It learns from emails that have already been labelled as spam or normal, then uses the words in a new email to estimate which class it belongs to.

Learning from labelled emails

The model looks at how often each word appears in spam emails and normal emails (using the bag of words method). It also learns the prior probability of each class: how common spam and normal emails are in the examples it was given. In this demo the two classes start equally likely, so the prior probability for each is 50%.

What is a bag of words?

The Naive Bayes classifier uses the 'bag of words' method. What does this mean? The bag of words method is the method of counting the amount of times a word appears (in this case in either the spam or normal emails) and adding it to a vector. Each position in this vector correlates to a word that appears in the emails. While each of the values at that particular index, is the amount that word appears. This is one of the most rudementary Natural Language Processing (NLP) methods.

Calculating the probability

When a new email arrives, the model starts with each class's prior probability. Then it considers each selected word and asks how likely that word is in spam compared with normal email. These likelihoods are combined to estimate the probability of each class: \( P(C \mid w_1,\ldots,w_n) \propto P(C)\prod_{i=1}^{n}P(w_i\mid C) \). The probabilities for spam and normal are normalised so they add up to 100%.

The model uses Laplace smoothing by adding one to each word count. This means that each word is defaulted to 1. Why do we do this? We do This due to the fact that if simply one of the words in a sample email has exactly zero instances in the bag of words provided, it would bring the samples probability to zero for the spam or normal classification (depending on what was being calculated for). incrementing each word by 1 at the very start mitigates this issue.

Why is it called “naive”?

Naive Bayes makes the simplifying assumption that each word contributes independently once we know whether the email is spam or normal. In the real world, its not so simple. Words rely on each other to provide important context. Even though the real world language contains more nuance, keeping algorithms simple where possible is always preferred; such as in this particular case.

This demo uses a tiny set of example word counts to make the calculation visible. A real spam filter would learn from many labelled emails and consider much more context.
